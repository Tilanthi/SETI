Referee report 1

I have read the manuscript as I would a submission to an astronomy/astrophysics journal. My assessment is that the paper contains a potentially valuable and publishable piece of work. The use of the ALMA archive for technosignature searches is worthwhile, the extension to 89.6–873.1 GHz is genuinely interesting, and the authors have made an unusually serious effort to expose the limitations of their search rather than simply report a set of EIRP limits. The survey comprises 443 spectral windows toward 88 stars in 81 systems, with 126 windows providing meaningful drift discrimination and 317 providing only a coarse channel-confined excess search. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf

I particularly welcome the injection tests, the attempt to empirically calibrate the spatial-control statistic, the explicit treatment of spectral response, the recovery of β Pictoris CO as a positive control, and the careful distinction between a survey trigger threshold and a completeness limit.

However, in its present form the paper is too complicated statistically and contains several important internal inconsistencies and methodological issues that need resolution before publication. Some are matters of presentation, but several affect how strongly the null result and sensitivity limits can be stated.

My recommendation is therefore major revision rather than rejection. I think the central observational result—no credible persistent technosignature in the searched data—is likely to survive, but the statistical framework and description of the survey need to be simplified and made more rigorous.

Major comments and required revisions

1. The spatial-control null is interesting, but the manuscript currently demonstrates that its central assumption is false. This should be treated more fundamentally. The original premise is that the statistic at the star can be ranked against 512 surrounding positions. The out-of-sample experiment subsequently shows that the ranks are not uniform: the held-out stars have median rank 0.44 rather than 0.50, with significant block- and star-clustered evidence for the discrepancy. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf Figure 4 then demonstrates a radial dependence of the control statistic and, critically, that the star generally occupies a radial region in which there are no control probes at all. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf This means that the fundamental exchangeability assumption is not merely imperfect; the control experiment was geometrically mis-specified. I agree with the authors that the empirical tail behaviour can still make the statistic useful as a screening device. But I recommend that the paper stop referring to the original construction as a statistical “null” except in the historical description. It is better described as a spatial candidate-ranking statistic whose false-positive behaviour has subsequently been empirically calibrated.
2. The proposed repair of the spatial null is itself not independently validated. This point needs particular attention. The manuscript states that the radial profile m(u) used to detrend the controls is measured from the held-out windows, and then assesses the behaviour of the detrended held-out distribution. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf That is effectively training and testing the repair on the same sample. The held-out sample is independent of the original survey, but it is no longer held out with respect to the repaired model once it is used to derive m(u). This should be corrected in one of three ways: derive the correction on one subset and validate it on another; perform star-/block-level cross-validation; or, preferably, avoid presenting the repaired statistic as independently validated at all and use the measured tail false-positive rate directly. Since the candidate dispositions apparently do not depend on this correction, the third solution would make the paper considerably cleaner.
3. I strongly recommend that the authors simplify the statistical narrative. The manuscript currently contains the original region-max statistic, the symmetric statistic, radius matching, radial detrending, pseudo-stars, a local scramble null, clustered permutation tests, recurrence, visibility-domain fitting, line masking and several post-hoc diagnostics. The transparency is commendable, but the result is difficult to follow even for a specialist reader. Table 3 is a useful start, but it should be expanded into one definitive analysis flow. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf I suggest defining only three levels in the main paper: detection threshold → empirical spatial screen → astrophysical/recurrence confirmation. Everything else can be labelled explicitly as validation or robustness testing and moved to appendices. The paper currently makes the statistical argument appear less secure simply because the reader has to reconstruct which version of the statistic is operative at which point.
4. The terminology “pre-registered” should be changed. The authors are admirably open that important elements of the analysis were developed after inspecting the original data: the symmetric statistic replaced the region-max statistic nine days after an AU Mic feature had been flagged, and the molecular-line mask was also modified after β Pictoris crossings were seen. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf That is acceptable in an exploratory archival study provided it is disclosed, but it is not conventional preregistration. The criterion used to assess the second CP−72 2713 block may have been prospectively frozen before looking at that repeat observation, but that is not equivalent to preregistering the primary survey analysis. I recommend phrases such as “prospectively frozen before examination of the repeat block” or “repository-timestamped before the validation sample was analysed”.
5. The extreme primary-beam corrections require much more stringent treatment. This is, in my view, a significant methodological concern. The paper retains a window at 1.58 \theta_{\rm PB} with a primary-beam correction of 1000.13×, while rejecting four ε Eri windows with corrections of only 2.9–3.4× because the Gaussian beam model is considered unreliable there. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf It is very difficult to understand how a correction of a factor of ~1000 can be considered quantitatively defensible for an ALMA primary beam. At that response level, tiny uncertainties in the beam model translate into enormous errors in inferred source flux, and sidelobes, pointing errors and beam asymmetries become important. The manuscript should either adopt a conservative primary-beam response cutoff—e.g. exclude positions beyond a specified gain threshold—or demonstrate through ALMA beam models that corrections this large are valid. I would not accept EIRP limits based upon a ×1000 correction. Even if these windows do not determine the headline sensitivity, they should not appear alongside reliable limits as equivalent measurements.
6. The ACA primary-beam geometry should be corrected in the actual analysis, rather than discussed retrospectively. The paper acknowledges that 141 windows come from the ACA 7-m array while the control geometry was defined using a 12-m primary beam. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf The authors argue that this acts conservatively and show that removing ACA data does not change the headline results. That is reassuring, but this is nevertheless a known geometric error in the operative statistic. Since the analysis is reproducible and the dataset manageable, I would prefer to see these 141 windows re-extracted with the correct 7-m primary beam rather than leave a known instrument-model error in the final survey. At minimum, the corrected ACA analysis should be provided as a formal robustness table.
7. There is an explicit inconsistency concerning the response-plus-smearing correction. The Abstract states that a “window-specific combined response and smearing correction is released with the catalogue”. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf Yet Appendix A states that when Hanning loss and intra-integration smearing are compounded, the worst penalty is ×4.6 and “no released column carries that product.” technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf These statements cannot both be correct. This must be fixed. I recommend adding a single catalogue quantity,
    P_{\rm eff}=P_{\rm trig}C_{\rm response}C_{\rm smear},
    computed individually for every window, and using that quantity consistently in the Abstract, Table 5, Figures and Conclusions.
1. Relatedly, the treatment of P_{\rm trig}, P_{\rm eff}, P_{50} and P_{90} remains unnecessarily confusing. Table 2 attempts to separate them, which is useful, but a reader is still confronted with nominal EIRP, effective EIRP, response-corrected power, smearing-corrected power and injection completeness. The manuscript itself emphasizes that P_{\rm trig} is not a completeness limit. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf I recommend that every sensitivity plot display only one principal physical sensitivity quantity, preferably the actual window-specific P_{\rm eff}, with completeness information shown separately. P_{\rm trig} is principally an internal pipeline threshold and need not receive nearly as much prominence.
2. The claimed factor ×2.29 Hanning correction deserves a clearer derivation in the main text and consistent application to the injection experiments. The authors correctly point out that the original injected tones were unsmoothed and therefore do not automatically include the spectral response penalty. Appendix B subsequently performs a separate response simulation and obtains ×2.02–2.69, median ×2.31, in excellent agreement with the analytic estimate. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf This is good work, but the presentation currently makes the reader discover rather late that the end-to-end injection campaign is not truly end-to-end with respect to the correlator spectral response. The distinction should be explicit where the completeness curve is first introduced.
3. The Class A/Class B division should be based primarily on the physical drift-resolution parameter rather than an instrumental channel-width gap. The authors themselves show that six Class A windows have \eta_{\rm drift}<1, while twelve Class B windows reach \eta_{\rm drift}\geq1. The classification is therefore only approximately equivalent to the stated physical distinction. The instrumental bimodality makes the adopted division operationally convenient, but the paper repeatedly describes Class A as “drift resolved” and Class B as not. For the handful of exceptions, this is not literally true. I suggest either reclassifying by \eta_{\rm drift}, or changing the terminology to fine-channel and coarse-channel, and then reporting separately whether each window actually resolves the searched drift.
4. The molecular-line mask needs a numerical consistency check. Table 9 gives 4.474 GHz removed from a 113.86-GHz unique-frequency union, i.e. 3.9%, not 4.4%. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf Yet §6.2 and the Conclusions refer to approximately 4.4% of bandwidth being removed. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf This looks suspiciously like confusion between 4.474 GHz and 4.4 per cent. The authors should check all occurrences. Since the line-mask exclusion is repeatedly emphasized as an important selection effect, the percentage must be correct.
5. I agree with the authors that masking molecular frequencies is scientifically conservative, but I would change the primary search strategy. Approximately 131 of the 443 windows contain a masked transition, and the authors explicitly acknowledge that these are natural “magic frequencies” at which an engineered beacon might deliberately transmit. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf A better approach is to search all frequencies, flag line-coincident candidates, and classify them using spatial morphology, recurrence, velocity behaviour, polarization and visibility-domain localization. The paper itself recommends this for a successor survey. I would not require the authors to redo the entire survey before publication, but I would encourage them to report the masked crossings in a parallel table rather than describe those frequencies simply as absent from the experiment.
6. CP−72 2713 should remain explicitly an unexplained event rather than be rhetorically grouped with noise. I think the manuscript actually handles this reasonably well in §5.3: the feature is at the stellar position, is point-source-like in a visibility fit, has a significant first-block measurement, and does not recur two hours later. The authors correctly state that two observations cannot distinguish a noise fluctuation from an intermittent emitter. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf That wording should remain consistent everywhere. The appropriate conclusion is not “false positive” but “an unexplained single-epoch event with no evidence for persistence and with a survey-level occurrence rate consistent with the measured false-positive distribution.” This is scientifically stronger because it says exactly what the data demonstrate.
7. The visibility-domain analysis of CP−72 2713 is arguably more physically meaningful than the original spatial-rank statistic and should be given greater prominence. The fitted visibility signal is localized at the stellar position with a real component consistent with a point source and an imaginary component close to zero. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf The authors themselves conclude that visibility-domain localization would be the first change they would make in a future survey. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf I agree. Since the primary statistic has a demonstrated radial-control problem, I would consider reprocessing at least all four stage-1 windows, and preferably all 20 crossing windows, directly in the visibility domain. That would provide a cleaner physical candidate test and reduce reliance on the imperfect spatial ranking.
8. The completeness analysis is much improved by the 28-configuration stratified injection experiment, but the headline statements need to reflect what has actually been calibrated. The 4,032-trial campaign is a strong addition: P_{50} varies from 0.64–1.13 of the median and 108/126 Class A windows lie within the sampled parameter space. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf However, 18 Class A windows remain extrapolations and environmental dependence is explicitly untested. This means that the phrase “measured Class A completeness” should be used cautiously. I suggest reporting sensitivity in three categories: directly calibrated, interpolated within the injection grid, and extrapolated outside the grid. This information could easily be another catalogue column.
9. The population-level implications should remain deliberately modest. I agree strongly with the statement that the conditional searched-domain transmitter fraction is not an occurrence rate. The sample is dominated by objects selected for disc/planet-formation science, with 67 of 81 systems associated with debris-disc selection, and it is strongly deficient in M dwarfs relative to the local census. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf Table 11 quantifies this particularly well: F and G stars are overrepresented by factors ~3–4, while M stars are underrepresented. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf I would actually remove or considerably shorten the illustrative population-frequency calculation in Appendix I unless it provides substantial information beyond the exposure accounting. The strongest scientific result is the searched parameter space and sensitivity, not a constraint on the prevalence of technological civilizations.
10. The comparison with centimetre-wave SETI surveys in Figure 1 remains potentially misleading despite the caveats. The manuscript correctly states that channel widths span about seven orders of magnitude and that a lower total-power EIRP threshold does not mean greater sensitivity to a hertz-wide transmitter. This warning is essential. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf I would go further. Either split the figure completely into two panels with an unavoidable visual link between EIRP and spectral resolution, or add a third representation normalized to a specified transmitter linewidth. Otherwise readers will inevitably reproduce statements such as “ALMA reaches lower EIRP than X”, which are not physically meaningful without channelization.
11. The polarization wording in the Abstract should be corrected. The Abstract says that use of Stokes I “costs up to ×1.41 in signal-to-noise on a fully polarised carrier.” technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf Elsewhere the paper correctly explains the subtler point: a fully polarized source contributes its full total power to Stokes I, but searching the favourable individual hand could improve S/N by \sqrt2. The present Abstract wording could be interpreted as saying that Stokes I loses 29% of the actual polarized signal, which is not the same statement. I suggest: “A search of the individual parallel-hand correlations could improve S/N by up to \sqrt2 for a fully polarized carrier; the present analysis uses Stokes I only.”
12. The manuscript is too long for the amount of primary scientific result and should be substantially shortened. Much of the current 31-page paper documents the history of the analysis rather than the astronomical experiment. The provenance is valuable and should remain available, but much of it belongs in a reproducibility appendix or repository. The main article should tell a much simpler story: sample → data → search → empirical calibration → four outliers → dispositions → sensitivity → limitations. Details such as commit hashes, failed historical statistics, intermediate masks and withdrawn tests need not repeatedly interrupt the scientific narrative. 13. I would restructure the Abstract. The present Abstract is impressively candid but reads almost like a referee-response summary: it mentions the non-exchangeable null, its repair, the number of blocks searched since freezing, stellar-type bias and polarization. The central astronomical result is obscured. Scientific judgement

I do not think the problems above invalidate the basic non-detection. Indeed, the authors’ own robustness tests make it quite likely that the four interesting windows have been correctly interpreted: β Pictoris provides a convincing positive control through recurrent CO emission; HD 48370 is consistent with foreground molecular emission; and CP−72 2713 is a marginal, non-repeating but genuinely unexplained first-epoch event. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf The independent 603-window extension producing a similarly marginal non-repeating event toward HD 23484 is also useful evidence that such events arise at roughly the expected false-positive rate. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf

The paper’s strongest contribution may ultimately be methodological rather than the numerical EIRP limit. It demonstrates both that archival ALMA technosignature searches are feasible and that apparently attractive spatial-control statistics can fail because of interferometric field geometry. That is an important lesson for future ALMA/NOEMA/VLA archival SETI searches.

The paper also contains a strong reproducibility component. Publishing the per-window results, injection trials, 512-position control statistics, code, masks and frozen analysis snapshot is exemplary and considerably increases the value of the work. technosignatures_40pc_v3.62.pdf

Referee Report 2

I read the full 31-page manuscript and wrote a formal referee report (attached). My overall take: this is a technically strong, unusually self-critical paper — the spatial-control null (measuring the false-alarm rate empirically against 512 surrounding positions rather than assuming it) is a real methodological contribution to interferometric SETI, and the β Pictoris CO positive-control demonstration is well chosen. I'm recommending minor-to-moderate revision.
The report covers five major technical points in detail:
1. The exchangeability repair (§5.4/App. H) — the authors find their control ensemble isn't exchangeable with the star, propose two competing repairs (radius-matching vs. detrending), and adopt one without fully justifying the choice or showing both side-by-side across all four flagged windows.
2. Real detection power — the paper is honest that no single window can reach survey-level significance given the Bonferroni correction, but the practical consequence (recurrence across epochs is what actually resolves candidates, not the rank statistic) deserves a prominent, quantified statement rather than being left in an appendix.
3. CP−72 2713, the one unattributed, non-recurring event — I flag that a solar-system-body/near-field crossmatch for that specific epoch isn't mentioned, and ask the authors to state their follow-up plans.
4. Sample bias toward F/G stars and its consequence for habitable-zone interpretability — currently buried in §6.3, should be in the abstract/intro.
5. Two sensitivity-degrading corrections (Hanning ×2.29, polarization ×1.41) that should be combined into one stated worst-case number.
It also has a full set of presentation notes (terminology overload, heavy inline bolding atypical of journal style, figure legibility, symbol reuse of "T" for four different quantities, a probable typo in Appendix I's Table 15 discussion) and 13 itemized minor/technical comments tied to specific sections, plus a "points to commend" section.

1. Summary of the paper
The authors mine the public ALMA archive for 88 stars in 81 systems within 40 pc, extracting 443 spectral windows (113.9 GHz of unique sky frequency) and searching each for narrowband spectral technosignatures using a Doppler-drift matched filter. Windows are split by native channelisation into a drift-resolved "Class A" search (126 windows, fine channels) and an unresolved persistent-excess "Class B" search (317 windows, coarse channels, no drift discrimination). The key methodological contribution is a spatial-control null: rather than assuming Gaussian/1-in-N statistics, the search statistic at the stellar position is ranked against the same statistic computed at 512 surrounding control positions, so the false-alarm rate is measured empirically. The authors discover this null is not exactly exchangeable (the control statistic varies systematically with radius from the pointing centre), diagnose the cause, and apply two candidate repairs, adopting a "detrending" correction for the released catalogue. Four windows survive first-stage screening; three are confidently attributed to circumstellar/foreground CO (two toward β Pictoris, one toward HD 48370, cross-checked against independently published CO detections and kinematics), and one (CP−72 2713) remains an unattributed, non-recurring, single-epoch event that the authors correctly decline to elevate to candidate status. No credible technosignature is reported. Extensive appendices document frequency/velocity conventions, injection-recovery completeness, the molecular-line exclusion mask, false-alarm bookkeeping, and a full analysis-provenance/robustness audit, and the pipeline, catalogue and control statistics are released via GitHub/Zenodo.

This is a careful, self-critical, and unusually well-instrumented piece of work — the spatial-control null, its exchangeability failure, and the two repair strategies are, to my knowledge, a genuine methodological advance for interferometric SETI, and the positive-control demonstration on β Pictoris CO is a good way to show the pipeline is not simply insensitive. My comments below are aimed at improving clarity, tightening a few statistical justifications, and making the paper's real scope (and its limits) easier for a general reader to absorb — not at the underlying result, which appears sound.

2. Major comments
M1. The exchangeability repair needs a more decisive treatment, not two competing patches
Section 5.4/Appendix H is, to my mind, the most important part of the paper, and it is currently the hardest part to follow. The authors find the 512-control ensemble is not exchangeable with the star (the control statistic depends systematically on annulus radius), propose two independent repairs — radius-matching (restrict to the 54 innermost controls) and detrending (subtract a radial profile fitted on held-out data) — report that they disagree on how large the residual displacement is (D = 0.066, p = 0.06–0.28 vs. D = 0.091→0.081, p = 0.32–0.60), and then adopt detrending "for the test below" without a first-principles argument for why detrending is preferred over radius-matching, or over doing both and taking the more conservative disposition.
I recommend the authors:
* State explicitly, in one place, why the physical origin of the radial trend (primary-beam response, scale ≈0.14–0.78 θ_PB) argues for one repair over the other, rather than presenting both as roughly interchangeable and picking one procedurally.
* Report survey-wide flagging outcomes under both repairs side by side (a version of Table 10 for the detrended vs. radius-matched statistic), so a reader can see whether the four stage-1 outliers and their dispositions are repair-independent. The paper states "no window is promoted by either repair," which is reassuring, but this robustness claim deserves a table rather than a sentence buried in Appendix H.
* Consider whether a parametric model of the radial primary-beam response (rather than an empirically measured profile on held-out windows) would remove the need for repair altogether, and if not, say explicitly why not (e.g., mixed 12 m/7 m array configurations, Appendix G).
M2. Multiple-testing power is discussed but the paper should state its detection limit for a real, non-recurring, moderate-significance signal
The authors are commendably honest that "no single event in this design can... reach survey-level significance" (§5.4) and that the per-window floor (1/513) sits a factor of 16 above the Bonferroni-corrected survey-wide threshold. This is an important limitation that deserves a more prominent, explicit statement in the Discussion: as constructed, a genuine but weak persistent transmitter recovered in only one epoch is statistically indistinguishable from the expected ~1 chance event per survey (as CP−72 2713 demonstrates), and the pipeline's real resolving power comes entirely from recurrence across independent epochs and from independent astrophysical attribution (line ID, kinematics), not from the rank statistic itself. I would like to see, quantitatively, what fraction of the 81 systems have more than one independent epoch available (Table 15 gives n_i > 1 for 18/81, "against 63 with two or more" — this sentence in §I appears to have a typo/ambiguity, see minor comments below) and hence which fraction of the sample the recurrence test can actually adjudicate. This number belongs in the main Discussion (§6.2 or §6.4), not only in the appendix, since it directly qualifies how strong the non-detection claim can be interpreted to be.
M3. CP−72 2713: rule out (or explicitly address) a solar-system or near-field origin
The one unattributed, non-recurring event is treated thoroughly as an astrophysical/instrumental question, but I did not see a discussion of whether a solar-system body (asteroid, TNO, or a near-field/terrestrial emitter reflected into the beam) was checked as a possible explanation for a single-epoch, non-repeating, channel-confined excursion. Appendix H's "exploratory diagnostics" (closure phase, RFI/near-field discussion in §4.5) address near-field emitters for the survey generically, but not for this specific window. Given this is the paper's one genuinely open case, I would like an explicit sentence stating whether a minor-planet ephemeris crossmatch was (or was not) performed for the CP−72 2713 field at the relevant epoch, and if not, a statement of why it is not needed. Relatedly, the authors should say plainly whether they intend to propose new ALMA observations to re-check this specific target, since a fresh epoch is the only way to resolve the ambiguity the paper itself identifies (§6.4 recommends this for a "successor" survey in general terms, but does not commit to or rule out this specific star).
M4. Sample characterisation and its astrobiological relevance should be foregrounded, not left to §6.3
The abstract and introduction correctly flag that the sample is F/G-enriched and M-dwarf-deficient, but the practical consequence — stated only in §6.3 — that "this null result carries very little weight for the habitable-zone or Sun-like-star question" is an important scope caveat that a reader skimming the abstract could easily miss, especially since SETI results are frequently over-interpreted in secondary reporting. I recommend adding one sentence of this kind directly to the abstract or the final paragraph of the introduction, alongside the existing "target-complete but epoch-incomplete" caveat.
Similarly, the finding that 53 of the 168 nominally ALMA-covered stars (31%) turned out, on closer inspection of per-block pointing, to contain no actual observation despite passing the initial field-of-view crossmatch (§5.2) is a significant data-quality lesson for anyone attempting similar archival crossmatches (e.g. with the VLA, NOEMA, or JVLA archives, as the authors note in §6.4). This deserves a slightly expanded discussion: was the over-generous quoted field of view specific to solar-system-target observations (as stated), and would a simple additional filter (e.g., excluding MOUS with solar-system intents) have caught most of these in advance? A brief "lessons for future archival crossmatches" paragraph would increase the paper's value to the wider community attempting similar archive-mining efforts.
M5. Combine the two dominant sensitivity-degrading corrections into one headline number
Scattered across the abstract, §4, and §6.2/6.4, there are (at least) two multiplicative corrections that both degrade the effective sensitivity to a real transmitter relative to the nominal 5σ trigger: the Hanning/spectral-response correction (median ×2.29) and the Stokes-I/polarisation penalty (up to ×1.41 for a fully polarised carrier). These are conceptually independent and, in the worst case, compound (≈×3.2 relative to the nominal per-channel 5σ threshold). I recommend the authors state this combined worst-case factor explicitly once, since it is the number a reader will actually want when comparing this survey's claimed sensitivity against, e.g., Enriquez et al. (2017) or Price et al. (2020) in Figure 1 — currently Figure 1 plots only the effective (already response-corrected) threshold, which is good, but the polarisation penalty is not folded into the plotted values or explicitly flagged as an additional uncorrected factor on top of what is plotted. Please state directly under Figure 1 whether the plotted EIRP thresholds already include, or still exclude, the polarisation penalty.

3. Presentation and structure comments
P1. Terminology density
Table 1 introduces eight specialised terms (crossing, stage-1 spatial outlier, p_rank,min, P(false flag), target-complete, epoch-incomplete, etc.) before the reader has seen a single result, and several of these terms recur with subtly different scope later (e.g., "stage-1 outlier" in §4.2 vs. the unrelated "second-stage local null" introduced only in Appendix G — the "stage" numbering across these two concepts is not obviously connected, and I found myself re-checking Table 1 repeatedly through §5). I suggest either (a) renaming the "second-stage local null" to avoid the implication that it is stage 2 of the same "stage-1" screening ladder, since it is a separate, later-introduced validation device, or (b) adding a short forward-reference in Table 1 pointing to where "stage 2" is defined. A single consolidated notation/terminology box (perhaps as a two-column table split logically into "selection vocabulary" vs. "statistical vocabulary") would help.
P2. Overuse of inline bold emphasis
The manuscript bolds complete sentences quite frequently within running prose (e.g., in the abstract: "No credible technosignature candidate survives..."; in §4.2: "443 windows are not 443 independent trials..."; in §5.4: "The control ensemble is therefore an empirically calibrated screen..."; similarly in §5.4, §6.2, Appendix H). This is more typical of a preprint/blog-style write-up than of AAS/OJA house style, where emphasis is usually carried by topic sentences and section structure rather than bolding. I would recommend the authors reduce this to at most one or two genuinely load-bearing statements per section (e.g., the non-detection statement in the abstract and the multiple-testing caveat in §5.4 are worth keeping bold; most others read fine as plain text) and defer to the journal's style guide.
P3. Figure legibility
* Figure 1 packs several literature surveys (Enriquez+17, Margot+23, Mason+24, Price+20, GBT/Parkes/Arecibo benchmarks) onto one small panel with many marker types; at typical journal column width the legend and marker distinctions (open/filled triangles, diamonds vs. circles) risk being difficult to distinguish in print/greyscale. Consider enlarging or splitting the legend, or moving the literature comparison to a table plus a simplified plot.
* Figure 3 is data-rich but very tall/dense (81 systems × 45 frequency islands); please confirm the star-name labels remain legible at final print size, and consider whether splitting into two panels (nearest/farthest half of the sample) would aid readability, with the full combined version retained in the online/repository materials.
* Figure 6 and Figure 8 are clear and effective; no changes needed.
P4. Symbol overloading
Track duration/on-source time is called "T" in Eq. (1) (η_drift = |ν̇_max| T/Δν_ch) while the search statistic is T(x) and its stellar value is T_* (Eq. 2, Table 1), and T_eff (via teff_gsphot) is used for effective temperature in §3/Table 11. While each use is locally unambiguous, the shared letter "T" for four distinct physical quantities (integration time, the matched-filter statistic, its per-window realisation, and effective temperature) adds unnecessary cognitive load in a paper that already introduces a lot of new notation. I recommend renaming the on-source track duration in Eq. (1) (e.g., to τ_track or T_on) to free up "T" for the statistic alone.
P5. Title/abstract numeric consistency
The title states "81 stellar systems," the abstract leads with "88 stars in 81 systems," and the sample-selection section subsequently discusses 168 candidate stars, 115 genuinely covered, and 88 actually searched. This hierarchy is well explained by Figure 2's funnel, but a first-time reader encountering the title and abstract in isolation (e.g., in an ADS listing or conference abstract book) may reasonably wonder which number is "the" sample size. I suggest adding a half-sentence in the abstract's second sentence clarifying that 88/81 is the searched sample drawn from a larger archive-covered set, to pre-empt this.

5. Points to commend
* The spatial-control null (measuring, rather than assuming, the false-alarm rate via 512 surrounding control positions) is a genuine and useful methodological contribution to interferometric SETI, and the paper's willingness to discover, diagnose, and transparently repair a flaw in its own null (rather than quietly folding it into a resubmission) is exactly the right way to handle this and should be commended, not just tolerated.
* The β Pictoris CO recovery, cross-checked against independently published transitions, velocities, and disc geometry (Matrà et al. 2017; Dent et al. 2014; Cataldi et al. 2023), is a well-chosen and convincing positive control demonstrating the pipeline is not simply blind.
* The injection-recovery campaign (Appendix B) and the fully independent out-of-sample check on 603 newly searched windows (§5.3) are good practice and materially strengthen confidence that the released false-alarm rate is not an artefact of the exact sample the statistic was tuned on.
* The paper is unusually honest about its own limitations (completeness bounds, sample bias, polarisation blindness, non-independence of windows, drift-ceiling scope) and consistently avoids overstating the non-detection as an occurrence-rate constraint — the explicit "conditional detection-probability" framing in Appendix I, and the refusal to quote a headline "fraction of the galaxy excluded" number, is exactly the right level of caution for this kind of result.
* Full pipeline, catalogue, and control-statistic release via GitHub/Zenodo under open licences is excellent practice and should make this a useful benchmark dataset for future archival SETI efforts on ALMA, NOEMA, or the JVLA.

6. Recommendation
I recommend minor-to-moderate revision. The core result and methodology are sound and represent a valuable contribution; my comments are aimed chiefly at (i) tightening the justification and presentation of the exchangeability repair (M1), (ii) stating the survey's real per-event resolving power and recurrence coverage more prominently (M2), (iii) closing the loop on the one unattributed event with an explicit statement about solar-system contamination checks and follow-up plans (M3), and (iv) a number of clarity/structure improvements that would make the paper considerably more accessible without weakening its rigour.
