ABSTRACT
Could a nearby star be running a narrowband millimetre transmitter bright enough that ALMA
has already recorded it? Almost nothing above 30 GHz has been searched, and no search there has
reached stars this close. We have therefore searched the public ALMA archive at each star’s own
position for an unresolved carrier whose frequency drifts: 402 spectral windows toward 60 stellar
systems within 40 pc, with channels fine enough to follow a drift, covering 47.7 GHz of sky frequency
in disconnected intervals from 90 to 873 GHz. Of the 56 threshold crossings, 19 fall on catalogued
molecular transitions, including the β Pictoris disc, recovered blind; the rest are no more numerous
than each field’s own control positions predict, though those positions are not a perfect null. Only 1
unattributed crossing is brighter at its star than at any control, and it is absent at the next observation
of that frequency. No carrier both passed the selection and recurred in an independent observation.
Sensitivity is measured by injecting carriers into the calibrated visibilities and recovering them through
the whole selection: the median system’s best window reaches 1.77 × 1015 W, the power at which nine
carriers in ten survive every stage — a recovery fraction, not a detection probability. Coarser windows
add a supplementary limit on unresolved spectral excess. What limits such a search is line confusion
and how often the archive revisits a star at one frequency, not sensitivity; and because ALMA chose
these stars, the result describes them, not the solar neighbourhood.

1. INTRODUCTION

This paper asks whether any nearby star is transmitting a narrow radio signal that ALMA happened
to record while observing something else. The public
archive holds thousands of hours of millimetre and submillimetre observations taken for quite different reasons,
mostly studies of dust discs and planet formation, and
every one of them also contains a spectrum of the star
at the centre of the field. A transmitter would appear in
such a spectrum as a spike confined to one or two channels. It would sit at the star’s position and nowhere else
in the field, and reappear if the star were observed again.
We searched the archive for exactly that. Nothing of the
kind survived.
A technological civilisation may imprint the electromagnetic spectrum detectably whether or not it intends
to, a technosignature (Sheikh 2020; Vidal et al. 2026),
and millimetre wavelengths are an attractive place to
look for one. Interstellar scattering and dispersion fall
steeply with frequency, so a high-frequency carrier arrives less distorted; a given aperture delivers higher gain,
and so more radiated power per watt supplied to the
transmitter; and coherent millimetre emission is already
engineered on Earth, for directional links, radar and
beamed power. The band is correspondingly hard to
search. The atmosphere transmits unevenly and adds
phase noise, receivers are noisier than at centimetre wavelengths, and archived correlator channels are thousands
of times coarser than a dedicated narrowband backend,
so a carrier is diluted within one channel and its drift can
smear it across several. These bands are also crowded
with molecular lines, a dense background of real astro1 probe

physical signals that a threshold search must separate
from an artificial one.
Six decades of radio searches have examined many
thousands of stars, yet sample only 6×10−18 of the space
of transmitter frequency, bandwidth, power and duty cycle (Wright et al. 2018). Essentially all of that effort lies
below ∼30 GHz. Above it we are aware of one published
search, in ALMA Band 3 (Mason et al. 2025), whose targets lie at 1.01 kpc and beyond; no search above ∼30 GHz
has yet reached a star within 40 pc at all. Few searches at
any frequency reprocess existing interferometric archives,
although those archives already hold a spectrum of every
target they have ever pointed at (§1). That is the regime
this paper enters.
The signal sought here is an unresolved spectral carrier.
It is emission confined to a few channels, unresolved at
the stellar position, and persistent enough to recur between epochs. Each qualifier is a real restriction. A
carrier broader than the channel width would not be recovered, nor one that fills the synthesised beam, nor one
that appears once and never again. Its frequency is allowed to drift, as it must for a transmitter carried on a
rotating, orbiting body, and the drift searched is bounded
by a line-of-sight acceleration rather than by a frequency
(§1). The fine channels used are 15.3–1953 kHz wide,
so “narrowband” here describes the assumed transmitter
and never the resolving power of the data.
One limit on interpretation holds throughout. The targets were chosen by archival disc and planet-formation
programmes, not for this search, and the sample inherits
their preferences in distance and in spectral type (§1).
Every statistical statement here is therefore about the
ALMA archive-selected stellar sample and not about the
solar neighbourhood.

2. CONCLUSIONS

1. ALMA’s public archive opens a region of search space
unexamined toward nearby stars. No search above
about 30 GHz has reached a star within 40 pc, and the
one previous Band 3 search targeted stars at 1.01 kpc
and beyond. Of the 118.1 GHz of unique sky frequency searched here in either class, 20.6 GHz lies
inside the frequency axis of the Wright et al. (2018)
search haystack, which defines a parameter space and
not coverage by any telescope.
2. The primary experiment is 402 fine-channel windows toward 60 stellar systems, covering 47.7 GHz of
unique frequency in disconnected intervals, searched
for an unresolved carrier whose frequency drifts. A
secondary coarse-channel search for an unresolved
spectral excess adds 70.4 GHz in 1249 windows, with
no drift discrimination. Both come from one uniform
reduction of 404 public execution blocks, 4.1 TB of
raw data.
3. Sensitivity is a measured completeness, not a threshold. Carriers injected into the real calibrated visibilities are recovered through the unchanged selection, the spatial ranking included, and EIRP90 is
the power at which nine in ten come back. It
is quoted per window: 3.45 Ptrig where the control
ring is noise, a bound where resolved disc emission raises it. The median system’s best window
reaches 1.77 × 1015 W, with a spread across systems
of 7.74 × 1013 –5.79 × 1016 W.
4. All 56 threshold crossings are reported with their
dispositions. Of these, 19 coincide with a catalogued molecular transition, almost all of them carbon monoxide. Of the remaining 37, 36 have repeat
coverage at the same frequency and were tested for
recurrence in the star’s own frame: none is present
again, the largest statistic at any predicted cell being 4.71 against Ttrig = 5, and the weakest exclusion
5.0σ. The one untested crossing had not retained its
discovery spectrum. Exactly 1 of them outranks all
512 control positions, which is a prioritisation and
not a confirmation.
5. What this survey could search and what it could
confirm are different extents and must not be read
as one statement. The search covered 60 systems,
of which only 30, carrying 44 per cent of the finechannel coverage, were observed again at the same
frequency more than a day apart, and 11 beyond a
year. A transmitter is persistent here if it would be
detectable above the quoted power in every independent observation covering its frequency.
6. Combining each star’s epochs in its own rest frame
deepens the flux limit by a median factor 1.46. For
Proxima Centauri the stacked limit, measured by injection into the stack, is 3.19× deeper than that star’s
own best single epoch and reaches 1.73 × 1013 W in
15.625 MHz channels — a Class B limit on an unresolved excess, carrying no drift discrimination — for
an emitter steady in the stellar frame to 20.9 km s−1
across 6.3 yr.
7. These results constrain persistent, unresolved carriers

within the frequency, drift, duty-cycle and sensitivity
domain actually searched, toward the stars ALMA
happened to observe, and not the solar neighbourhood. The more transferable result is what such a
search runs into. Thermal sensitivity does not bind
it — ALMA supplies more than the experiment can
use — but molecular-line confusion, a channelisation
chosen by other observers for other purposes, and
how often the archive happened to look twice.
